Ontario residential brokerage: market, rules, competitors, and where Klyr can win

Research on GTA market conditions, TRESA and RECO requirements, and the offers from Keypr, Zown, Wahi and flat-fee brokerages. Ends with a recommended position, pricing model and launch plan.

KEY TAKEAWAYS

  1. The rebate war is crowded. Keypr returns 80% of the buyer-side commission; Zown up to 1.25% of price (max $25,000); Wahi has offered 1%. Competing on rebate size alone puts Klyr in a race to zero.
  2. Every competitor stops at the commission. None shows the buyer the full cost to close (lawyer, inspection, insurance, mortgage rate) or lets those providers compete for the job. Klyr already has this in the prototype.
  3. TRESA rewards a software-led process. Written buyer agreements, the RECO Information Guide, financial-benefit disclosures and multiple-representation consent are all required steps. A product that builds them into the flow turns compliance into trust.
  4. The market favours price-sensitive buyers right now. GTA sales were down 9% year over year in September 2026, average price $1,006,409. The new-build HST rebate (up to $130,000, until March 31, 2027) is pulling some buyers to new construction.
  5. Recommended position: “The lowest all-in cost to buy a home in Ontario, with a full-service agent.” A flat buyer fee with the rest rebated (no lender or lawyer strings), plus a competitive quote marketplace for every closing cost.

One economist quoted in Now Toronto suggested it may be shifting buyers from resale (via Wahi press page). Builders reported a sales jump after launch (Globe and Mail). Implementation details (APS wording, assignments, lender treatment) were still being clarified mid-2026 (Real Broker Ontario).

What this means for Klyr (inference). Buyers have more choice and more time, so cost and confidence matter more than speed. A new-build lane with HST-rebate guidance is a time-limited opportunity worth testing before March 2027.

2. How brokerage is paid in Ontario

  • Commission is negotiated, not regulated. The convention is 5% split 2.5% / 2.5% plus 13% HST, paid by the seller, but rate, split and payer are all negotiable (ExamAce). Observed 2026 totals range 3.5–5% (ExamAce).
  • Brokerages may not collude to fix commissions or imply a “standard” rate (source). Klyr copy must avoid “the standard 2.5%”.
  • Under TRESA the buyer agreement must state how the buyer’s brokerage is paid. If the buyer can’t pay, their agent can negotiate a seller-paid clause in the APS (RECO TRESA FAQs). The co-op offered by a listing is not guaranteed, so a rebate must be calculated on what Klyr actually receives.
  • Rebates to a brokerage’s own buyer client are legal if disclosed and paid by the brokerage; commission can’t be paid to an unregistered third party (Zown, citing TRESA).

4. Rules the product must follow (TRESA / RECO)

RECO is the regulator that enforces TRESA. OREA is the REALTOR® association that publishes the standard forms (for example the Buyer Representation Agreement) and member guidance.

Client or self-represented, nothing in between
TRESA removed the “customer” category. Giving advice or services can create an implied agreement (REM, citing RECO Bulletins 2.3, 2.6, 2.7).
Product: label every in-app tip as general information until the buyer agreement is signed.
Written buyer agreement
Must be in writing, signed by the brokerage and given to the buyer before an offer; must set term, services, remuneration and termination (REM, O. Reg. 567/05 s.13.6). Industry guidance treats it as required before showings (ExamAce).
Product: current gate before the first visit is correct. Rename “RECO Form 300” to “OREA Form 300” in the prototype.
RECO Information Guide
Must be provided before services, to clients and self-represented parties alike (checklist, OREA).
Product: show and log acknowledgement at sign-up.
Financial benefits
Any benefit an agent may receive from a referral must be disclosed as soon as known, with best efforts to get acknowledgement (RECO Bulletin 3.3).
Product: every provider quote shows whether Klyr earns anything from it.
Multiple representation
Representing buyer and seller in one trade requires written disclosure and each client’s consent (RECO).
Product: auto-detect when a Klyr buyer views a Klyr listing and trigger consent.
Advertising
Brokerage named clearly with “brokerage”; claims accurate and verifiable; promises of savings and rebates are scrutinised (Bulletin 5.1, 5.3).
Product: “Klyr Realty Inc., Brokerage” on every screen footer; savings calculator shows assumptions.
No paid help for self-represented sellers
Brokerages must not enter agreements to assist self-represented sellers or charge for it (RECO FAQs).
Product: the seller app must be a representation service, not a “FSBO toolkit”.

5. Recommended position

The lowest all-in cost to buy a home in Ontario, with a full-service agent and every step on one timeline.

Keypr owns “biggest rebate”. Zown owns “down payment help for first-timers”. Neither owns the total cost or the closing experience. Klyr’s prototype already covers what they don’t: lender, lawyer, inspector and insurer quotes, and a day-by-day plan tied to condition periods.

PILLAR 1
Flat fee, no strings
Klyr keeps one flat buyer fee; the rest of the commission is rebated. Same amount whichever lender or lawyer the buyer chooses, unlike Zown’s tiered offer.
PILLAR 2
Closing-cost marketplace
Licensed providers quote side by side, with Klyr’s financial interest disclosed on each. The buyer sees one “cost to close” number.
PILLAR 3
Full-service, fewer hours
A real agent for showings and negotiation; the app handles scheduling, documents, conditions and check-ins. Positioned between Keypr (self-directed) and traditional.
PILLAR 4
Compliance as a feature
Buyer agreement, Information Guide, disclosures and consents are signed in-app and stored. Marketed as “every promise in writing”.

Why not just beat 80%? Keypr keeps about $5,000 for ~15 agent hours. A full-service agent needs more time. Klyr’s pitch is a rebate within ~$1,500 of Keypr plus a real agent, plus savings on lawyer, inspection, insurance and mortgage that Keypr doesn’t surface.

6. Target customers

  • First-time buyers, 28–40, GTA condo and townhouse. Most price-sensitive. In Zown’s Angus Reid survey, 42% of respondents chose a cash-back-style incentive as the most helpful support (Business Wire).
  • Newcomers. Unfamiliar with the Ontario process, so the step-by-step timeline and plain-language conditions matter most. Zown already targets this group.
  • Move-up sellers who also buy. Klyr can represent both sides of their own move (list and buy) with one fee schedule, which rebate-only startups can’t.
  • New-build buyers until March 2027. HST-rebate checker in the app; coordinate with a Klyr lawyer on APS rebate wording.

8. Risks

  • Falling co-op commissions. If listings offer less, rebate-led models weaken. A flat fee is easier to explain than a percentage of an uncertain number.
  • Provider fees as conflicts. Referral income from lenders or lawyers must be disclosed under Bulletin 3.3 and could undermine the “lowest cost” claim. Consider flat platform subscriptions instead of per-deal referral fees.
  • Lender treatment of rebates. Some lenders restrict how rebates count toward the down payment. Confirm with partner lenders before promising “at closing”.
  • Advertising claims. Comparative savings against named competitors must be accurate and current.
  • Capital. Zown and Keypr are funded and already have track records. Klyr needs a tight launch geography.
